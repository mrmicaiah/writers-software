# CHAPTER 1

I didn't know it at the time, but it was my turn to die.

Not because I was a bad guy. I certainly don't feel like a bad guy. I try to do right by the people around me. I love my little boy, even when he begs me to come to this crazy theme park.

The line for the Dominator wrapped around twice. The sun was beating down on my shoulders and arms, and I could feel my skin starting to sizzle. I should've put on the sunscreen. I could hear my wife's voice in my head: *You're gonna want the sunscreen.* And I could hear my own voice drowning her out: *Mary, please. I don't like that stuff.*

But I should've known better. Like it or not, I was an accountant, and with that comes a particularly pale skin, apparently.

The two girls in front of me talked incessantly about some boy who was treating their friend badly. Had I known I was going to die, I probably would've told them to shut up.

But I didn't. Like a lot of things I wished I'd done with my life, here was another one.

I was a nice guy. I couldn't help it. Even if I wanted to get mad and angry, I could never make myself do it.

The coaster arrived again, and we stepped under the awning. The shade felt like mercy.

"Daddy, I don't want to ride this anymore."

Jacob's voice cut through the crowd. I looked down at him. His hand was wrapped around my fingers, squeezing tight.

The ride conductor stepped forward. "Sir, do you not want to ride?"

I looked at him. He was young — college kid, probably. He was looking at me like I'd insulted him personally. I could see it in his eyes. He wanted me to get on. He had a quota, a line to keep moving, a job to protect.

Jacob tugged at my sleeve.

I looked down at him. His eyes were wide. He didn't want to be here anymore. Neither did I, if I'm honest. But we'd waited forty minutes, and the line behind us was restless, and the conductor was staring, and I was a nice guy who couldn't make himself say no.

"Stand over there by the coat rack," I said. "I'm gonna go ahead and ride."

The conductor guided him over. Jacob stood there clutching a metal railing, watching me. I sat down behind the two girls. They were laughing, enjoying life. I was cramming my legs under their seat, trying to get comfortable in a space designed for someone smaller.

I couldn't help but think about how I'd gotten here. My time working. The skills I'd obtained over the years. All of which meant very little to anyone around me at that moment.

The ride lunged forward.

Jacob waved goodbye to me. He didn't know he was waving goodbye for the last time.

The coaster inched up the first hill. I wasn't a heavy man, but I could feel a weight settling on my chest. I felt scared. Anxiety gripped my heart and was beating it like a bad drummer. Something was wrong.

I looked over to motion to the conductor, but we were too far forward. The platform was already shrinking below us.

We climbed higher. The weight got worse.

I tried to scream, but I couldn't. I couldn't breathe. My lungs wouldn't expand. The restraint pressed against my shoulders like a hand holding me down.

I looked out at the crowd of people below — tiny figures milling around, eating funnel cakes, checking their phones, living their lives. And there, standing still among all that movement, was a man in a blue polo shirt.

He was looking right at me.

Staring. Like he knew something I was about to find out.

The coaster crested the hill. The girls in front of me threw their hands up. Everyone around me screamed with joy.

I couldn't make a sound.

Then, all was dark.

---

For a moment, the world was perfect.

The execution was complete. And for all intents and purposes, everyone simply saw a man who died riding the Dominator at Kings Dominion amusement park. Exactly how it was supposed to be. Similar to an artist at Pixar, dreaming as they think to themselves: *If we do our job right, nobody will ever notice.*

Standing up from a bench, he made his way through the crowds of people.

He wasn't sure why the man had to die. He didn't ask that type of question. He didn't need the information. He needed the *who*, and he was very precise in ensuring he got the identity correct. He never considered his moral compass to point north, but he did have one, and taking out the right person was important.

His file on Clyde Garrison was short, like all of them. Name. Birthdate. Clear pictures of his face from all sides. Social security number. Current address. Phone number. Two people he hung out with the most. This was all used to find and verify he had the right person. Then, through a very anonymous channel, the target was introduced to his maker.

The sun set lazily on the horizon as the perfect stranger made his way through the crowd being directed to the parking lot. The evening glowed blue and red with the small herd of police cars near the entrance. No one had seen him. No one had noticed. No one ever noticed, and he liked it that way.

He didn't need to brag to anyone. He didn't need recognition. He neither desired nor wanted a spotlight of any kind. Content to dwell forever in the shadows, like a spider in the house of a king — doing as he pleased to whomever, and for whoever paid the most.

Climbing into his car, he slipped into the line of vehicles leaving the parking lot and disappeared.
