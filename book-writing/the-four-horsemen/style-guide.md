# THE FOUR HORSEMEN — WRITING STYLE GUIDE

## **FOUNDATION**

This is a psychological thriller in the Grisham tradition. Clean prose. Dialogue-driven scenes. Grounded realism. Slow burn tension. The style guide from *The Realtor* applies here with the modifications noted below.

---

## **LANGUAGE & PROFANITY GUIDELINES**

### **ACCEPTABLE LANGUAGE:**
- Hell, damn, bastard, ass (sparingly)
- "God" (not as expletive)
- "For God's sake" / "God knows"
- Son of a bitch (only in extreme moments)
- Pissed, screwed, crap

### **NEVER USE:**
- Jesus Christ (as expletive)
- F-word or derivatives
- C-word
- Extreme sexual references
- Blasphemous combinations
- Modern slang profanity

### **CHARACTER-SPECIFIC LANGUAGE:**
- **Ryan:** Rarely curses — too controlled. Maybe one "damn" when everything falls apart.
- **Professor Stanfield:** "Damn it" when frustrated. Mutters to himself. Uses formal language even when angry.
- **Detective Pickler:** "Hell" freely. "Son of a bitch" when something clicks. Blue-collar vocabulary.
- **John Que:** Never curses. His restraint is part of his menace. Speaks like a father, even when threatening.
- **Emily:** Clean language. Grounding presence. "Oh my God" in shock (the shooting).

---

## **POV & TENSE RULES**

### **THE POV SHIFT (OPENING ONLY):**

The cold open uses **first person, past tense** for Garrison's death on the coaster. This is the only first-person section in the book. It ends with "Then, all was dark."

Immediately after, the book shifts to **third person limited, past tense** for the rest of the novel.

**Why this works:**
- First person pulls the reader inside a dying man's head — intimate, claustrophobic
- The shift to third person feels like a camera pulling back — he's gone, the world keeps moving
- The contrast is ice cold

### **POV CHARACTERS:**
- **Ryan Gauntlet** (~70%) — Primary POV. Reader experiences most of the story through him.
- **Professor Stanfield** — Cutaway POV. His sections show the machine behind the deaths.
- **Detective Pickler** — Cutaway POV. His sections show the investigation closing in.
- **John Que** — NEVER a POV character. Experienced only through others. This preserves his menace.

### **POV RULES:**
- One POV per scene, no head-hopping
- POV established in first line of each scene
- Internal thoughts in italics, used sparingly
- Cutaway scenes are SHORT — get in, deliver the beat, get out
- Ryan's long stretches carry the emotional weight

---

## **PSYCHOLOGICAL DEPTH — THE BALANCE**

This book needs more interiority than standard Grisham — Ryan's performance anxiety, the Professor's guilt and obsession, Pickler's stubborn fixation. But we don't sacrifice pace for psychology.

### **THE RULE:**
Psychology is revealed through **action and dialogue**, not narration. When we do go internal, it's tight — one or two sentences, then back to the scene.

### **EXAMPLES:**

**TOO MUCH:**
> Ryan sat in his car, replaying every name he had reported. He thought about Garrison, about the man on the spreadsheet, about the way Que had said "tragic" with no feeling behind it. He wondered if he was complicit. He wondered if he had always known. The weight of it pressed down on him, and he felt something shift inside, something he couldn't name but knew would never shift back.

**RIGHT:**
> Ryan sat in his car. He pulled up the spreadsheet on his phone and scrolled through the names.
> 
> Three of them were dead.
> 
> He put the phone down. His hands were shaking.

**THE DIFFERENCE:**
- Show the action (scrolling, counting, shaking hands)
- Let the reader feel the weight without explaining it
- Trust the reader to understand what this means

### **WHEN TO GO DEEPER:**
- Transformation moments (Ryan leaving Emily bleeding, picking up the gun)
- Mirror moments (Professor realizing what he's become)
- The interview scenes we've drafted (Ryan's voice document material)

Even in these moments, keep it tight. One paragraph maximum, then back to action.

## **GRISHAM STYLE ELEMENTS**

### **PROSE STYLE:**
- Clean, direct sentences
- Active voice predominant
- Minimal adjectives/adverbs
- Show through action, not description
- One metaphor per chapter maximum

### **CHAPTER STRUCTURE:**
- Start in scene, no weather/setting preambles
- End with hook or revelation
- 3,000-3,500 words average
- Multiple short scenes over single long ones
- White space between scene breaks

### **DIALOGUE RULES:**
- Sounds like real people talk
- "Said" is invisible — use it
- Interrupted sentences show tension
- Professional jargon used accurately but sparingly
- Regional dialect suggested, not spelled out

---

## **DIALOGUE ATTRIBUTION**

### **USE SPARINGLY:**
- whispered
- shouted
- muttered
- asked

### **NEVER USE:**
- exclaimed
- queried
- responded
- interjected
- pontificated

### **INSTEAD OF ATTRIBUTION:**
Use action beats:
```
"The money's gone." Ryan's hands shook.
"All of it?" Emily stepped back.
```

---

## **SCENE CONSTRUCTION**

### **EVERY SCENE NEEDS:**

1. **Purpose** (not written, just known): "This scene shows X"
2. **Opening:** Jump into action/dialogue
3. **Conflict:** What's the problem? Want vs. obstacle
4. **Resolution:** Win, lose, or draw — but create new problem
5. **Exit:** Hook or question. Make reader turn page.

### **CHAPTER OPENINGS — ROTATE:**

**Type 1 — Dialogue:**
> "You don't look like someone who steals," Que said.

**Type 2 — Action:**
> Ryan scrolled through the names. Three were dead.

**Type 3 — Short Statement:**
> The coaster didn't stop.

**Type 4 — Internal:**
> He had been giving them names for six weeks.

---

## **CHAPTER ENDINGS**

### **END EACH CHAPTER WITH:**
- Question
- Revelation
- Decision
- Arrival
- Discovery
- Threat
- Promise

### **NEVER END WITH:**
- Sleep
- Driving away
- Thinking
- Waiting
- Description
- Memories

---

## **DESCRIPTION GUIDELINES**

### **CHARACTER DESCRIPTIONS:**
- One defining physical trait
- Revealed through action when possible
- Clothing only if it matters to story
- Age shown through context

**NOT:** "Professor Stanfield was 58, balding, with nervous hands and a paunch from too many late nights."

**INSTEAD:** "Stanfield's briefcase was organized like a soldier's kit. Pens in rows. Highlighters by color. Everything in its place."

### **SETTING DESCRIPTIONS:**
- Two sensory details maximum
- Function over beauty
- Weather only if it affects action
- Architecture only if relevant

## **TECHNICAL ACCURACY**

### **MUST BE ACCURATE:**
- Corporate structure / financial crimes
- Detective procedure (Virginia jurisdiction)
- University professor life / tenure
- Assassination methods (plausible, not graphic)
- Money transfers / offshore accounts
- Fredericksburg and Northern Virginia geography

### **CAN BE VAGUE:**
- Specific poison chemistry
- Exact police protocols
- Capital Investments corporate details
- The Horsemen's full operation

## **THE GARRISON THREAD**

Garrison's death connects all three POV characters. His name must surface naturally throughout:

- **Act One:** Professor confirms the kill. Small moment — a pause, a drink — signals this one was different.
- **Act Two:** Pickler interviews the Professor about Garrison. The memory surfaces. Reader learns the Professor knew him.
- **Act Three:** At his lowest, the Professor connects his downfall back to Garrison. First domino.

Garrison is never heavy-handed. He's a thread, not a theme. His name appears, the reader feels it, we move on.

---

## **THE WOLF STORY**

Two versions, two fathers, two children:

- **Que tells Katie:** Oliver the Wolf and Little Red Riding Hood. A father who IS the wolf, warning his daughter.
- **Ryan's father told him:** A darker version. "She was an idiot... there are wolves out there and they run the woods."

The wolf story is **not explained**. It's told, it echoes, it haunts. The reader decides what it means.

---

## **THEMES (SUBTLE)**

### **PRIMARY:**
- Intelligence isn't street smarts
- The system consumes everyone — even the people who built it
- Transformation has a cost

### **SECONDARY:**
- Fathers shape their children in ways they don't intend
- Performance becomes identity
- There's no clean exit from dirty work

### **NEVER PREACH:**
- Themes emerge through action
- Characters don't state themes
- Reader discovers meaning

---

## **MOMENTUM RULES**

### **PACING TECHNIQUES:**
- Short sentences in action scenes
- Longer sentences in reflection
- Paragraph breaks for emphasis
- Single-line paragraphs for punch
- Time jumps clearly marked

### **THE TICKING CLOCK:**
- Professor's contract ending in weeks
- Street killer in custody — clock on the Professor
- Que's deadline for the Ryan kill
- Show time pressure without stating it

---

## **COMMON PITFALLS**

### **DON'T DO:**
- Dream sequences
- Extensive backstory chapters
- Philosophical monologues
- Experimental structure
- Unclear timeline
- Passive protagonists
- Coincidental solutions

### **ALSO DON'T DO:**
- Info dumps about assassination methods
- Psychology lectures
- Rich/poor stereotypes
- Love at first sight (Ryan/Emily is earned)
- Explaining the wolf story

---

## **THE GOLDEN RULE**

**"Would Grisham write this sentence?"**

If the answer is no, revise.

Clean. Direct. Compelling. The reader should forget they're reading and simply experience.

---

## **DAILY WRITING CHECKLIST**

Before writing each chapter, confirm:

- [ ] Clear POV established first line
- [ ] Start in scene, not summary
- [ ] Conflict on every page
- [ ] Dialogue sounds natural read aloud
- [ ] End with momentum
- [ ] Advanced plot, character, or both
- [ ] No weather openings
- [ ] No mirror descriptions
- [ ] No profanity beyond guidelines
- [ ] Time/date clear if it matters
- [ ] Psychology shown through action, not explained
